The destination
Inte-Run in the App Store · full Apple Watch and Garmin support · a coach that notices when your running says the plan should change, and asks before changing it · a community that matches you with runners of similar ability nearby.
Three rules for the whole journey: finish one phase before starting the next · ship small and often rather than big and rarely · anything involving other people's data or safety is never the corner to cut.

What a real coach told us

Jump the queueno cost

A GB international 10km runner — an elite coach — used the app and sent six things back. Every one was checked against the actual code and every one was real; three turned out to be worse than he described. This is the most valuable feedback the project has had, because it is the difference between a plan that looks right and one a coach would stand behind.

He kept having to re-enter things. The form only held what you typed on screen, so anything that refreshed it put your old answers back — and simply tabbing out of the last question did exactly that, every time. Fixed.
Your race is not in the plan at all. With a Sunday race the app puts a long run on race day itself; with a Wednesday race it schedules a recovery jog on the day and the long run four days AFTER you have raced. Worse, on some combinations of race day and long-run day it puts hard intervals the day before. Race day belongs at the end, with nothing after it and nothing hard beside it.
Your call, and it is the same argument that removed the cool-down four days ago: an “ease in” at easy pace, at the front of a run that is easy the whole way through, is the same effort wearing a different label. A 37′ recovery jog was actually taking you 45 minutes because of an 8-minute warm-up in front of it. Now what the card says is what you do — a 32′ run is 32 minutes, an 80′ long run is 80 minutes of running. Tempo and interval sessions keep their full warm-up, because those ask you to run hard from the first repetition. So does a beginner on walk/run sessions — you asked for their brisk walk specially, and it stays.
When you added your own easy run you could only dial it in minutes. You can now choose either — there is a “Measure by” switch, so a 6 km easy is two taps. Switching between the two keeps the run you had: dial 40 minutes, flip to distance, and it offers you the same run in kilometres rather than starting again.
The rule was written but it missed one day of the week. If your race is on a MONDAY, the day before is the Sunday of the previous week, and the app only ever looked inside race week — so it never saw it. A Monday marathon had a 98-minute long run the day before it. The test had the same blind spot, so it agreed the rule was working. Both fixed, and the day before a race is now a gentle 20-minute shakeout rather than nothing at all.
If you pick "just getting started" or "building the habit", the plan never looks at what you signed up for. Training for a half marathon, your longest run in the whole 28 weeks is 4.4km — then you are sent out to run 21.1km. It is the same 4.4km whether the goal is a 5k or a marathon. The beginner plan is gentle, which is right, but it has to arrive somewhere. Fixed: the beginner long run now knows what you entered and builds toward it — training for a half you reach 12km instead of 4.4km, for a 10k you reach 8km instead of 4.5km, and the run-walk plans roughly tripled too. Two things kept it safe: the build-up is now the same small percentage every week rather than the same number of minutes (the old shape put a 17% jump at week five, right where a new runner can least take it), and a beginner half deliberately stops short of the full 21.1km — the research says 12-18km for a first-timer, whose job is to arrive able to finish, not to have already done it. Checked against what beginners can actually enter — "just getting started" is capped at 5k and 10k and "building the habit" at a half, on purpose — and every one of those now lands in the range the research gives. The jump you face on race day dropped from 3.7x to 1.4x for a first 10k, and from 5.0x to 1.8x for a first half.
Everyone got the same 110 minutes for a half-marathon long run. A 1:25 runner covers 22.4km in that time; a 2:30 runner covers 12.8km — 61% of the race they are about to attempt. Minutes are the right way to measure effort, but there was no distance floor underneath. Fixed: the plan now works out how long YOUR easy pace takes to cover the distance your race and level call for, and uses whichever is longer. For a half marathon that means going PAST the race distance, which is what the research and your coach both say — everyone up to about a 2:00 half now does. Slower runners get as far as 2 hours 25 carries them and no further, on purpose: reaching 21.1km would take a 2:30 runner over three hours, and that is a worse mistake than a slightly short long run. Note this is a half-marathon rule, not a general one — nobody runs past 42.2km in training for a marathon. Checking the marathon turned up the same fault in both directions: slower runners topped out at 19km (the research links anything under 25km to a slower marathon) while a 2:30 marathoner on high mileage was being sent out for 44.4km — further than the race itself. Both fixed. Every ability from a 2:30 to a 5:30 marathon now lands in the right range, and the longest session in any plan anywhere dropped from 44.7km to 33.9km. Your own marathon plan peaking at 28.2km is correct, and deliberately so.
The app had only ever kept your AVERAGE heart rate — one number, which cannot be drawn as a line. Both recorders now save a reading every five seconds, paired with how far you had run at that moment, so the debrief shows the shape of the effort: on an intervals session you can count the reps off the chart. Your watch also now sends four things it never sent — your highest heart rate, how long you spent in each training zone, calories and how much you climbed. It costs about 2KB a run, so it will not fill your phone. Three honest limits, all deliberate. A treadmill run gets no line, because it records a real clock and no distance — there is nothing to draw it against, and the distance you type in afterwards is a total that cannot say which beat happened where. If your watch loses contact with your wrist it leaves a GAP rather than a line: the watch just stops sending readings without saying so, and the first version of this drew the last beat it saw straight across the rest of the run, which looked identical to a steady effort — a strap loosening 20 minutes into an hour would have made up two thirds of the chart. And runs already in your logbook have no readings saved, so they show zones but no line.

The app has a good symptom checker, but it lives inside Ask Alfie and nothing connects it to the plan. Someone with chest pain or a suspected stress fracture can build a plan and start a run without being asked anything. The research is clear that safety questions come first, before any plan is made.
Support › Injury & symptoms was ten tick-boxes and a list of professionals to see. It is now a plan you can follow: first a short screen for the things nobody should look after themselves — a limb that looks wrong, one that is cold or numb, not being able to put weight on it — and then what to do in the first day, the first three days, the first week and the first fortnight. It ends with the first walk–run and the checks to pass before you do it. Two things in it go against the usual advice on purpose. Ice is for comfort, not healing — there is no good human evidence that cooling repairs tissue faster, so the page says so rather than repeating what everyone else says. And the calendar is not permission: two weeks is a point to review, not a green light, and the honest test is how the leg feels the next morning. There are no medicine doses anywhere, and it never tells you what you have — a tick-box cannot tell a pulled calf from a blood clot, and being told “probably a strain” is how people stop looking for help. Updated later on 10 August 2026: the page now opens with PRICE — Protect, Relative rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation — because that is what the NHS recommends for the first two to three days, and the first version of this page had wrongly left the name out. Every letter is explained, the R says plainly that it is not lying still, the ice timings are spelled out (wrapped, ten to fifteen minutes, never past twenty, at least two hours apart), and it still says none of the ice, wrap or propping-up has been shown to actually mend tissue faster. It also says out loud that you do not have to wait three days before you start moving gently. ⚠️ One thing for you: your sign-off covered the earlier wording. This PRICE version came from the updated briefing paper’s own clinical re-audit, and that paper still asks for a clinician to read the final page — so the new first-aid wording has not itself been signed off yet.
Support › Fuelling & energy was eight tick-boxes about not eating enough. It is now a guide to actual food: what to eat for the session you are doing, what to eat before a race, whether you need anything while running, how to drink, and what helps afterwards. Ordinary supermarket food first, with a plant version of everything; gels and sports drinks treated as tools for specific sessions rather than as the answer. The “am I eating enough” questions are still there, tucked into one section, and they now ask all twelve questions instead of eight — four of them had no box at all, so you could tick everything on the screen and still not have described what was happening to you. It also fixed four things the app had been saying that were simply wrong, the most important being about salt. The old advice was to drink something with sodium in it “rather than plain water”, which reads as though the salt is what keeps you safe. It is not. The danger on a long day is drinking more than you sweat out, and a sports drink can be over-drunk exactly like water — so every mention now says that in the same breath. Approved by you on 10 August 2026 after clinical review.
Hold a session row on the Plan screen for a moment and it lifts with a buzz — same as the calendar view you already use. Drag it onto a different day and it drops there with another buzz. The plan updates immediately, reminders move with it, and the watch sees the change. You can still tap the row normally to open it; a drag and a tap feel different, so they never get in each other’s way.
You can hear a coach before you pick one. The “choose your coach” step listed four names and gave you no way to tell them apart — now each one has a Preview button, so you choose by ear rather than by guessing from a description. The 2 km test date is a proper calendar now. It used to be a drop-down of days “this week”, which meant you could not put the test where you actually wanted it; tapping the date now opens your phone’s own calendar picker. And if you pick a day that already has a run on it, it asks first. Before, it would just land the test there. Now it tells you what is in the way, moves that run to a free day, and puts the 2 km where you asked — with an Undo if you change your mind.
The app was deleting your history. It kept only your last 50 runs — about twelve weeks — and then threw the rest away permanently, with no backup. It now keeps a small permanent record of every run you ever do: about 63 bytes each, so five years of running costs around 65 KB. Your maps and GPS routes still stop at 50, because the route is the big part.

That was also making your streak wrong in the worst possible direction. Because it counted from those 50 runs, it got shorter the more you ran. Tested with a real 40-week streak: it was showing 11 weeks, and someone running every day topped out around seven. Now it says 40.

On the back of that, the Logbook now opens with this week — distance, time and climbing — over a twelve-week graph, and a Training log button. Inside that is a month calendar showing what kind of run each day was, easy, quality or long, with your streak and how many runs a week you have been averaging. Tap any run to open it. There is also a fuller Progress screen with 7 days through to a year.

Two places deliberately refuse to guess. The calendar will not scroll back past your first ever run, because a blank day means "you rested" and it would be inventing rest days you never took. And runs older than your last fifty still show their distance but cannot be opened — the map and splits genuinely are not there, so the app says so rather than giving you a dead end.

Training zones — your heart-rate zones, and a box to put your real maximum heart rate in. That box needed building: the app has been reading that number in six different places and telling you on every run to "add your measured max heart rate in your profile", when there was no such box anywhere in the app. Saving it now also updates your watch, which colours your heart by zone from the same number.

Pace calculator — pick a race and a target time, get the pace. It also works backwards. One thing worth knowing: it always rounds the pace down. A half marathon in 1:45 needs 4:58.6 per km, and if it rounded that to 4:59 and you ran it exactly you would finish in 1:45:08 — the calculator would have given you a pace that misses the time you asked for.

Measurements — Celsius or Fahrenheit. Distance stays in kilometres for now; changing that properly means more than swapping labels, because your runs are stored in kilometres and both the phone and the watch measure a split every kilometre. It is on the list as its own job.

The old injury check-in had four questions that were not about injuries at all: worries about your eating, periods stopping, struggling mentally, and thoughts of harming yourself. Replacing that screen with the injury guide took the tick-boxes away with it. Nothing behind them was deleted — the app still recognises all four if you type them to Alfie, and still routes the serious ones straight to real help rather than trying to coach them. But typing is a higher bar than ticking a box, and those are the four questions where the bar should be lowest. They need somewhere to live. Two smaller jobs go with it: the eight new warning signs added for limb injuries work in the engine but nothing on screen offers them yet, and two places still say “a proper symptom checker” is at that address when what is there now is a leg-injury guide.
The age box accepts 12 upwards and the answer never reaches the plan, so a 15-year-old is given exactly the same marathon block as a 40-year-old. The guidance for young runners is different in kind, not just smaller numbers — it needs a grown-up and ideally a coach involved. Simplest honest fix is to say the app is for adults for now.
A 10km does not need the same wind-down as a marathon — as he put it, "the taper is not really a taper for a 10km". He was right, and it was worse than it sounded: the 5k and 10k "taper" was just race week itself, so how much easing you got depended on which day you raced — a Sunday race got six days, a Monday race got NONE, and the week before ran at full volume either way. Fixed twice over: every distance now gets the wind-down the research asks for (7–14 days for 5k, 10k and half; 14–21 for the marathon, whatever day you race), and the easing now applies to the whole week — before, only the long run shrank while the easy runs stayed full length, so the "taper" only cut about a fifth of the week instead of the recommended two-fifths. The sharpness stays: every taper week keeps one short, fast session, because resting the legs and dulling the edge are different things.
"Total mileage for a competitive runner looks a little on the low side." He is right: there is no mileage model at all. The app never asks your current weekly distance, and the one place in the code that could hold it is never read — you could say 20km or 160km a week and get an identical plan. Fixed, in two goes. First the app started asking, and a bigger week built a bigger plan. But a check against the research showed the answer was still being thrown away by anyone who runs LESS than the plan's usual size — three runners saying 10, 25 and 40km a week all got the identical plan, opening at 35km. Now your first week is built around what you actually run: it lands within 10% of your answer for 9 runners in 10, up from about half. The plan can shrink as well as grow, and when it shrinks it drops a hard session rather than only the easy running — otherwise a smaller week is a harder week, which is how people get hurt. One honest limit remains: past roughly 130km a week it settles short, because that mileage needs two runs in a day.
"How long do you run easy for, then how long do you steady for, over the course of 32km?" A marathon long run used to be one undifferentiated block. Now it reads like a session, on the screen and on the watch: warm up, a long easy stretch, then real work — some weeks a fast finish at race pace, some weeks blocks of race pace with short floats between (where race-day fuelling gets practised), some weeks a progressive run through three gears. The mix changes week to week, big efforts only appear in the build and peak, and easy weeks stay properly easy. Two safety rules came out of checking it: every dose is capped (a three-day-a-week runner was being handed 57 minutes at race pace at the end of a four-hour run — now 30), and on four running days or fewer a long run carrying race-pace work counts as one of the week's hard days, so the week around it backs off. A happy side-effect: 5k and 10k long runs used to carry chunks at full 5k race pace, which nobody could actually run — gone.
Carbohydrate per hour, roughly how often that is a gel, and practising it in training so your stomach is ready on race day. The app had one generic line and never mentioned it when you were actually about to run for two hours. Fixed: open any long session and the numbers are there for THAT run — how much an hour, how much in total, roughly how many gels' worth, when to take the first one and how often after. It scales with the run, and a marathon-pace long run is fuelled like the race itself — same 60–90g an hour, but using the exact gels and timings you plan to race on, because that is the part worth rehearsing. Two things it always says: above about 60g an hour, mixed sugars absorb better than one type on its own, and build up gradually because your gut trains like your legs do. Updated 10 August 2026: the 70–90g figure from your coach has been dropped back to the 60–90g range everyone else uses — starting a rehearsal at a rate you have never practised is the opposite of what the same card tells you to do. And it no longer blames one type of sugar for a bad stomach: high amounts, strong drinks, heat, pace and how much you drink all play a part, so there is more than one thing to change if it happens to you. Worth knowing: this is the one area your research report doesn't cover — it sticks to training — so these numbers come from standard sports-nutrition guidance and from your coach, not from the report.
Every session used to get much the same warm-up, whatever it was. Now the warm-up is built from the first hard thing the session asks of you and how soon it arrives: a run that starts easy is told the opening minutes ARE the warm-up, while a session that opens with fast repetitions gets the full version with drills and strides. Two faults you found on your phone are fixed with it. A 40-minute easy run with a quicker finish was being told "the quicker running starts early" and given strides — but the quicker part begins 24 minutes in, so it needed neither; the app was measuring the wait from the wrong point and ignoring the run's own gentle opening. And the warm-up was being ADDED to the run instead of being its first few minutes, so a 40-minute run quietly became 46. Both now come out exactly as long as the plan says.
The briefing used to tell you the pace and the effort for each part of a run but never how long you would be in it, so you had to work out in your head when the next bit arrived. Now every section carries its own time and distance — the warm-up, each drill, the strides, the work, the cool-down — and they are read off the session you are actually given rather than worked out separately, so they cannot disagree with it. Checked against every run the app can produce, including all four ability levels: it found nine faults, all fixed.
You watched a "38-minute moderate run" and found it spent 29 minutes at moderate — the warm-up and the wind-down were being taken out of the 38. You asked for the number to mean the work, and to grow the sessions rather than rename them. Now a 38-minute moderate run gives you 38 minutes at moderate with the warm-up on top, and the clock on your screen restarts when the warm-up ends, so the number you watch counting up IS the work. Warm-up jogging no longer counts as training mileage either — your point, that preparing the body is not training load because you can go as slowly as you need — so the weekly distance you told us still means what you answered. Finishing it turned up something considerably bigger, which nobody had noticed in the whole life of the app: the easy runs never got longer as the plan went on. A midweek easy run was the same length in week one as in your biggest week, so a 55km-a-week plan opened at 93% of its own peak — that is not a build-up, it is a flat line with a wind-down on the end. It also meant your "long run" could be the shortest run of the week. Easy runs now grow through the block the way the long run already did: your first week lands within 10% of what you told us for 9 runners in 10, up from 8, and the biggest week of a plan is now in the peak phase where it belongs for 97 plans in 100, up from 77. One knock-on: it showed the 5k wind-down was shallower than it claimed to be — the biggest week in a 5k plan used to be week 3 of 31, which flattered the comparison and hid it — so that has been deepened rather than the standard being lowered to match.
You asked for the prescribed cool-down jog to go, replaced by stretches you can choose to do, with your demonstration video at the top. Done. Tap "Stretch it out" on the screen after any run — or on the same run reopened from your logbook — and you get six stretches with hold times, a figure showing each one, a running clock and a Start button that counts you through them, left side then right. There is a slot waiting for your video. The rule that came out of building it is the useful part: a run that finishes easy loses its cool-down, a run that finishes on hard work keeps its jog. An "ease down" at the end of an easy run was the same easy pace under a different name — that is gone. Ten minutes of jogging after maximal intervals is a real change of effort — that stays. Checking it over found three sessions that would otherwise have ENDED on a sprint: easy plus strides finished on a 20-second stride, easy plus hill sprints on a maximal hill sprint, and moderate plus strides on an 80-metre stride, each with nothing at all after it. All three now walk back at the end, and it comes out of the easy running so no session got longer. A happy side-effect: the long-run problem below improved more from this than from anything else tried — it is now better than it was before any of this work started. You went out with the app and sent five screenshots back. Two were serious. Your phone was recording 0.00 km for the whole run — the app ignores tiny GPS wobbles so a phone sitting on a table doesn't invent distance, but it was applying that rule to every single reading, and it was set to half the accuracy of the signal. At the accuracy your phone had, each reading needed you to move more than 7 metres in a second — about a 2:22/km pace — or it was thrown away. At your actual pace nothing ever counted, so no distance and no map, and only a fast runner in perfect conditions would ever have recorded anything. It now trusts your phone when your phone reports how fast you are moving, and only falls back on the wobble rule when it does not. The protection is still there: a phone lying still records nothing, tested both ways. And the clock read 0:00 for the whole warm-up — the same change that made the named time the work time was hiding the warm-up entirely, so you could not see how much of it you had done. It now counts your warm-up up under a "Warm-up" label, then starts again from zero when the real work begins. Also: the clock was ticking in two-second jumps when your watch was recording, because the watch only speaks to the phone every two seconds to save battery — the phone now fills in the gap itself and corrects to the watch each time it hears from it. Plus your two tidy-ups: the gap under "Finish run" was too tight, and the bar down the side of a logbook entry now fades out instead of stopping dead. One more, on the watch: the word "Paused" was being clipped by the top corner of the screen, which is fixed but needs your eyes to confirm — a rounded screen is not something the Mac can check.
The first fix went the other way and you caught it in one run: 0.14 km without moving. My mistake, and it is worth writing down — I made the app trust the speed your phone reports, on the reasoning that if the phone says you are moving at walking pace then you are. A phone lying still reports a speed anyway, because it works its speed out from the same wobbly signal as its position. So every wobble counted. What it does now cannot be fooled either way: the app remembers the last spot it believed you were at, and only adds distance once you are genuinely further from it than the signal accuracy — jitter never gets that far from a fixed point, but you do the moment you actually run. Checking that turned up a third fault before you saw it: adding up every reading makes distance run LONG, because the wobble adds on each one — a test measured 124 metres of real movement coming out as 215. Measuring from the remembered spot fixes that too. One visible trade: your distance now steps up every 10 to 15 metres rather than counting perfectly smoothly. Your pace is unaffected. And there is now a "gps" line in Support › Your data showing how many readings were used and how many were rejected, because "0.00 km" and "0.14 km standing still" look exactly the same in a screenshot — next time it can be settled with numbers.
Found while measuring the change above, and it is a real fault rather than a rounding one: in about a third of all weeks a midweek easy run is longer than that week's "long run". A long run that is not the longest run of the week cannot do the job it is there for. Measured properly across 19,200 weeks, so the size of it is known rather than guessed: it was already happening in a third of weeks before any of this, got worse (two in five) when the sessions grew, and is now back to a bit over a third. Two causes, both understood. The long run begins its build-up much further back than the easy runs do, so in the early weeks it is allowed to be the shortest run of the week. And the long run loses its warm-up minutes from the mileage count while an easy run of the same length does not, which makes it look smaller than it is. Which of those to change is a coaching decision about how gently a long run should start, so it is coming to you rather than being guessed at.
He runs doubles on a Tuesday. Decided: the app will not schedule doubles, on purpose. Your research report is blunt about it — two runs a day are "reserved for verified high-performance athletes with extensive history and professional oversight", must "never be unlocked from self-selection", and its own test case says an app must NOT give doubles to someone who simply ticks "elite". Inte-Run deliberately refuses to award elite status from a questionnaire (it caps at "highly trained"), so it can never honestly establish who qualifies. Building it would mean either breaking that rule or asking you to prove race results to an app. What has been done instead is honesty: past about 100km a week the plan cannot fit the mileage into one run a day, and it now SAYS so on your plan rather than quietly handing you less than you asked for — and if you are only running three or four days, it tells you to add a day rather than a second run, which is the right advice for almost everyone. The other half of his point IS fixed: the app offered seven days a week and quietly built six, while still counting that seventh day when it told you whether your goal was realistic — so it was promising on the strength of a day it never gave you. Ask for seven now and you get seven, with the extra one a short 30-minute recovery jog rather than another full easy run, because seven days means no rest day and that session is there to keep the legs turning over, not to add more miles. A seven-day week is now genuinely bigger than a six-day one.
Typing into the profile page could shove the whole app up under the clock and leave a dead black gap between the buttons and the keyboard. The phone slides the view to reveal the box you tapped, a beat before the app makes its own room — and nothing slid it back. The first fix didn't hold on the real phone (this step was ticked too early — untick it if your map shows it done). Second attempt: the app now puts the view back the moment the phone moves it, and if the phone refuses, the app moves itself to match instead. This is now PROVEN rather than hoped: the Mac can drive a simulated iPhone with a real on-screen keyboard, and the measurements showed the phone shoving the view up by 280 points and refusing to give it back — so the first fix genuinely could not have worked — while the second catches it every time. That simulator test now runs before any keyboard change ships. And the design changed on the owner's feedback: the keyboard now simply covers the bottom of the screen like every other app — nothing slides up, nothing moves — instead of the app squeezing itself into the space above it. The box you are typing in always stays visible. There was one last gremlin — the phone itself jolts the view the instant the keyboard starts opening, faster than any app can react — so the app now cancels that shove in the same instant it happens, and the test watches the whole keyboard animation frame by frame rather than just the end. Confirmed fixed on the real phone.
The screen after a run is now one scroll that tells the story of it: your route, then the numbers, then the coach's read, then what the plan had actually asked for, then your heart rate, then somewhere to write a note. Fixing it turned up something worse than a missing feature. The screen you see in the minute AFTER finishing was throwing away what the plan had asked for — so even after a proper session it told you it was "a run by feel, nothing to judge it against", drew your pace chart with no target band, and hid the on-target and effort badges. Open the very same run from your Logbook a minute later and all of it was there. Two bits of code building the same run two different ways, and the wrong one was on the screen that matters most. Three more silent faults came out with it: every run recorded on your WATCH was losing its splits, so a wrist run got no pace chart, no splits table, and a share card whose "fastest km" printed as nonsense; every watch run was captioned "today" forever, because nothing ever recomputed the word; and the "how hard did that feel?" question only existed on the finish screen, so if you skipped it in the moment you could never answer it — and that answer is half of what the coach watches for. Today the same thing is published twice: as a website and inside the iPhone app. That's the cause of the “my change hasn't appeared” confusion you keep hitting, because the two update at different times. Publishing only the app removes that for good, and lets the app use phone-only features without keeping a web fallback. Important: this does not mean rewriting anything. Your app is already a thin Apple wrapper around the same screens the website uses — that's the design, and it's why there's only one set of screens to keep right. You chose this over a second maps account, for a good reason: a tester will not move off the app they already use if it means losing their Strava log. The hard part was never Strava; it was our own data. The intelligent coach

This is the flags system you described — and it doesn't have to wait for the App Store, because it's pure training logic. Version 1 already exists: the app re-estimates fitness from your runs and asks before changing anything. This phase completes it.
